> about01

I work at the intersection of three worlds: the research bench, the startup space, and the venture desk.

In the lab I build wearable biosensors and rapid diagnostics. As a founder, I've learned that even the best science seldom leaves the bench due to a lack of translation strategy, so I spend as much time with clinicians, investors, and grant committees as I do in the lab. On the venture side, I help evaluate which early healthtech has real technical substance and is commercially viable.

The thread through all of it is the gap between a discovery and a patient. That's the gap I'm trying to close.

> what I'm betting on02

COVID turned biosensors into a gold rush. Everyone shipped a test, and within two years the space was saturated with single-purpose, single-analyte devices, most of them commoditised the moment they launched. It was a live demonstration of how fast a single point-solution gets competed out of business.

So the model itself has to be rethought. A biosensor built for one target and one disease is a thin business hard to defend. The durable value is in the platform underneath. A customisable and flexible transduction core that can be retargeted to other disease models. A foundry for biosensors, not another single-point device.

This is the rationale I also employed in my research. The same nanoplasmonic-microfluidic core was retargeted across nucleic-acid diagnostics, antimicrobial resistance, respiratory infection, and wearable inflammation monitoring. One platform, many disease models.

The catch and what most "platform" pitches miss is that you earn platform economics by winning a sharp beachhead first. That's the thesis I build, and evaluate through.

> how I evaluate deep tech05

# the questions I ask before I believe a technical company

  1. IP at the coreIs the defensibility real? What's actually filed, how strong are the claims, and is there freedom to operate — or is the "moat" a pending provisional?
  2. A real beachheadEarly commercial signal — LOIs, paid pilots, design partners — de-risks a deep-tech company more than any deck. One customer who will pay beats ten who might.
  3. A team that covers the basesResearch spin-outs are usually technically deep and commercially thin. Who owns go-to-market, regulatory, and reimbursement — not just the science?
  4. Capital aimed at customersToo many technical teams fund the lab and starve the commercial motion, then stall at the first adopter. Is the capital plan built to reach buyers, not just the next milestone?
  5. Founder track recordHas the founder navigated the whole arc before — a prior exit or M&A? It signals they can carry a company past the science.
